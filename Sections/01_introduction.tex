\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Cognitive systems operating over extended periods must do more than perform an isolated task. They must decide what to learn, protect useful knowledge, allocate limited computation, act before opportunities disappear, and preserve resources for future operation. These demands interact: additional inference may improve a decision while delaying it, and protecting an established representation may preserve competence while impeding adaptation. The problem is therefore not simply to identify desirable outcomes, but to explain how a system regulates these competing demands over time.

Existing research already addresses important parts of this problem. Active inference relates perception and action through generative models and epistemic and pragmatic value \citep{friston2010fep,parr2022active}. Resource-rational analysis examines the allocation of limited computational resources \citep{lieder2019resourcerational,griffiths2015rational}. Homeostasis and allostasis describe regulation of viable internal conditions and anticipation of future demands \citep{billman2020homeostasis,schulkin2019allostasis}. Safe reinforcement learning explicitly incorporates risk and constraints \citep{garcia2015saferl}. These approaches show that uncertainty, resource limits, and viability are established concerns, not considerations absent from optimization-based accounts.

The Unified Variational Intelligence Framework (\uvif{}) proposes an organizing decomposition that brings five regulatory influences into a common description: epistemic pull, selective protection, computational drag, action efficiency, and sustainability. Its motivating question is whether keeping these influences explicit improves the explanation, diagnosis, or design of persistent cognitive systems. We use \emph{force} for a contribution to state dynamics, not necessarily a physical force. We use \emph{Variational Equilibrium} for an admissible stationary configuration of specified dynamics and distinguish it from the broader maintenance of viable operation.

\begin{principle}[Adaptive viability]
\label{prin:veq}
For some cognitive systems, explicitly modelling the interaction of information acquisition, protection, computational cost, effective action, and future resource availability may explain adaptation under changing conditions more effectively than models that omit those interactions.
\end{principle}

This hypothesis neither states that all intelligence requires exactly five components nor rules out an optimization-based implementation. Persistence alone is also insufficient to establish intelligence: a passive stable system may persist without learning or flexible action. The proposed framework concerns regulation within systems that already exhibit cognitive capabilities.

\subsection{Contribution and scope}
The paper makes three contributions. First, it defines a five-component organizational vocabulary and gives a dimensionally compatible dynamical formulation with explicit constraints. Second, it separates the proposed contribution from related cognitive and control frameworks, including cases in which the formulations coincide. Third, it develops hypotheses and an evaluation protocol that could test whether the decomposition adds predictive or engineering value.

This is a conceptual synthesis, not a systematic review or an empirical validation study. The literature is selected to motivate the components and examine neighbouring approaches; no exhaustive search or evidence-ranking procedure is claimed. The analytical example establishes a property of one stipulated model only. No experiments or simulation results are reported, and cross-domain examples are analogies and design scenarios rather than independent confirmations.
