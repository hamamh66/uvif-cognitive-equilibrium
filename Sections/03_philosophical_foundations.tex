\section{Natural and Conceptual Foundations}
\label{sec:foundations}

\subsection{Regulation in biological systems}
Homeostasis concerns the regulation of internal conditions that support physiological function \citep{billman2020homeostasis}. Adaptive homeostasis describes transient changes in regulatory capacity in response to signals \citep{davies2016adaptive}, while homeodynamics emphasizes changing patterns of organization rather than residence at a single fixed state \citep{lloyd2001homeodynamics}. Allostasis adds anticipation: resources can be mobilized in response to predicted demand rather than only after a deviation occurs \citep{schulkin2019allostasis}.

These accounts motivate a distinction between immediate performance and preservation of the capacity to perform later. They do not establish that biological regulation instantiates the five \uvif{} components as separable mechanisms. Biological systems can also fail, become trapped in maladaptive states, or persist under conditions that are undesirable for the organism. Natural persistence is therefore a source of hypotheses, not a guarantee of optimality, resilience, or ethical acceptability.

\subsection{Ecological resilience and the limits of analogy}
Ecological research describes resilience through feedback, reorganization, and responses to disturbance \citep{folke2006resilience,levin1998ecosystems}. This motivates studying operating regions and transitions rather than only average performance. However, the size of a basin of attraction is not a universal measure of resilience: conclusions depend on which variables, disturbances, timescales, and functions are considered. A persistent ecological regime may also be undesirable from a conservation or human-welfare perspective.

Mapping ecological processes to epistemic or pragmatic terms requires particular care. An ecosystem need not possess a single policy, a unified goal, or a model of its environment. Such translations can organize a research question, but they do not by themselves demonstrate cognition at the ecosystem level or a common mechanism across scales.

\subsection{What is variational about the proposal?}
In mechanics, a stationary-action formulation and local equations of motion can provide related descriptions under appropriate assumptions. Stationarity of an action does not imply that the physical state is stationary or asymptotically stable. Variational methods can also be used to derive optimization algorithms \citep{campos2021discrete}; they do not oppose optimization as a class.

The name \uvif{} retains the motivating idea of interacting variations in regulatory demands. The general vector-field formulation below is not derived from a common action functional and need not be a gradient flow. A strict variational interpretation applies only after a functional and the relevant geometry have been specified. Section~\ref{subsec:example} supplies a restricted potential-based example and makes its equivalence to constrained optimization explicit. Establishing a more general variational derivation remains an open problem.

\subsection{Variability, adaptation, and normative choices}
Variability can support exploration and adaptation, but its consequences depend on the environment and the mechanisms that retain useful changes. Genetic variation provides one biological example \citep{barrett2008adaptation}; it is not evidence that stochasticity always improves cognitive regulation. Deterministic controllers may also adapt to changing inputs. The present framework does not rely on a contrast between deterministic failure and stochastic resilience.

Similarly, ``nature-aligned'' describes biological inspiration rather than a normative criterion. Human designers and affected stakeholders must specify which functions deserve protection, which resource costs matter, and what outcomes are unacceptable. The framework makes these choices visible but cannot determine them from persistence alone.
