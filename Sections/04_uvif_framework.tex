\section{The \uvif{} Organizational Framework}
\label{sec:uvif_framework}

\subsection{Overview of the \uvif{} Architecture}

\uvif{} models an intelligent system as a dynamical entity embedded in
a structured force field. Rather than specifying a single objective
function, the framework identifies \emph{five fundamental forces} that
jointly govern the system's behavior, together with a set of
\emph{admissibility constraints} that bound the region of legitimate
operation. The configuration in which these forces balance defines the
system's \textbf{Variational Equilibrium} (\(\VEq\)).

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Figures/Fig4_UVIF_Force_Architecture.png}
\caption{UVIF variational force architecture. The framework represents
intelligence as the balance of multiple competing forces rather than
the maximization of a single objective. Epistemic uncertainty drives the
system toward better knowledge, protection preserves safety and
integrity, computational cost limits excessive complexity, action
inefficiency penalizes delayed or ineffective decisions, sustainability
protects long-term viability, and risk accounts for potential adverse
outcomes. Variational Equilibrium is the stable operating region where
these forces remain jointly balanced.}
\label{fig:uvif_force_architecture}
\end{figure}

Fig.~\ref{fig:uvif_force_architecture} presents the basic architecture
of \uvif{} in simple terms. The central circle represents
\emph{Variational Equilibrium}: the region in which the system remains
stable, useful, and viable. The surrounding forces show that
intelligence is not produced by one dominant objective alone. Instead,
the system must continuously balance the need to learn, the need to
protect itself and others, the cost of computation, the urgency of
action, the presence of risk, and the requirement of long-term
sustainability.

This figure also clarifies why \uvif{} differs from conventional
optimization. In a standard optimization view, the system is usually
pushed toward minimizing one error or maximizing one reward. In the
\uvif{} view, no single force should dominate completely. Excessive
learning may be too slow or intrusive, excessive protection may prevent
useful adaptation, excessive computational complexity may become
impractical, and excessive action speed may become reckless. The
intelligent condition is therefore the balanced state where the system
can continue to operate adaptively under uncertainty and perturbation.

Formally, let \(\mathcal{S}\) denote the state space of the intelligent
system and let \(\mathbf{s}(t) \in \mathcal{S}\) be its state
trajectory. The dynamics of the system are governed by:
\begin{equation}
  \dot{\mathbf{s}}(t) = F\!\bigl(\mathbf{s}(t),\, t\bigr)
    := \Epull - \Drag - \Prot + \Eff + \Sus,
  \label{eq:uvif_dynamics}
\end{equation}
where the five terms on the right-hand side are the Epistemic Pull,
Computational Drag, Selective Protection, Action Efficiency, and
Sustainability fields, respectively, evaluated at state $\mathbf{s}(t)$
and time $t$. The system is said to be in Variational Equilibrium when:
\begin{equation}
  F\!\bigl(\mathbf{s}^*,\, t\bigr) = 0 \quad
  \text{and} \quad \mathbf{s}^* \in \Adm,
  \label{eq:veq}
\end{equation}
where $\Adm$ denotes the admissibility set (Section~\ref{subsec:admissibility}).

This formulation is deliberately abstract; in Sections~\ref{subsec:epistemic}
through~\ref{subsec:sustainability} we give each force a concrete
interpretation and motivate its form. The power of the formulation
lies not in any particular mathematical instantiation but in the
\emph{relational structure} it imposes: no single force dominates, each
force constrains the others, and the equilibrium that results is a
property of the system as a whole, not of any individual component.

\begin{definition}[Variational Equilibrium]
  A state $\mathbf{s}^* \in \Adm$ is a \emph{Variational Equilibrium}
  of a \uvif{} system if the net force at $\mathbf{s}^*$ is zero and
  the equilibrium is stable: there exists a neighborhood
  $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{s}^*)$ such that for all
  $\mathbf{s}(0) \in \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{s}^*)$, the trajectory
  $\mathbf{s}(t)$ remains in $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{s}^*)$ and converges
  toward $\mathbf{s}^*$ as $t \to \infty$.
\end{definition}

In practice, Variational Equilibria will often be \emph{manifolds}
rather than isolated points---reflecting the homeodynamic picture of
biological stability \citep{lloyd2001homeodynamics,petitjean2025homeodynamics}.
A system may move along the equilibrium manifold in response to slowly
changing external conditions without losing stability.

\subsection{Epistemic Pull and the Drive Toward Understanding}
\label{subsec:epistemic}

The first force in \uvif{} is the \textbf{Epistemic Pull}
(\(\Epull\)), which captures the system's intrinsic drive to reduce
uncertainty about its environment, its own states, and the
consequences of its actions. Epistemic pull is the engine of
learning: without it, the system would not gather new information,
form new representations, or update its models of the world.

In humans, this drive manifests as curiosity---the intrinsic
motivation to explore and understand that has been shown to activate
reward circuitry in the brain and to enhance memory formation
\citep{ligneul2018curiosity,dubey2019curiosity}. In the free-energy
framework, the epistemic value of an action is its expected reduction
of uncertainty---the information gain that comes from sampling a novel
state of the world \citep{friston2017curiosity,parr2018generalised}.
\uvif{} incorporates this insight as a force, not an auxiliary
objective: epistemic pull actively moves the system toward states
from which uncertainty can be reduced.

Formally, \(\Epull(\mathbf{s})\) is proportional to the gradient of
expected information gain with respect to the system's state:
\begin{equation}
  \Epull(\mathbf{s}) \propto
  \nabla_{\mathbf{s}} \;\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathrm{IG}(\mathbf{s})\bigr],
  \label{eq:epistemic_pull}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{IG}(\mathbf{s})$ denotes the information gain
achievable from state $\mathbf{s}$, measured, for example, as the
reduction in entropy of the system's uncertainty distribution.

The epistemic pull is not unbounded curiosity. It is balanced by the
other forces, particularly computational drag and selective protection,
which prevent the system from pursuing information at ruinous cost or
at the expense of its protected internal states.

\subsection{Selective Protection and Controlled Disclosure}

The second force is \textbf{Selective Protection} (\(\Prot\)), which
represents the system's imperative to preserve its critical internal
structure---its core representations, its long-term knowledge, its
fundamental operating parameters---against corruption by noise, by
adversarial interference, or by the pressure of short-term optimization.

In biology, this force is instantiated at multiple levels: the blood-brain
barrier selectively filters what reaches neural tissue; the immune
system discriminates self from non-self; epigenetic mechanisms protect
developmental programs from stochastic variation \citep{farnsworth2025setpoint}.
In information theory, selective protection maps onto the distinction
between \emph{core} information that must be preserved and
\emph{surface} information that can be updated freely.

In an artificial intelligent system, selective protection governs which
parameters are frozen or regularized heavily (protecting accumulated
knowledge) and which are allowed to adapt freely (enabling ongoing
learning). It prevents catastrophic forgetting by imposing asymmetric
plasticity: high protection for foundational knowledge, lower protection
for recently acquired representations.

\subsection{Computational Drag and Resource Constraints}

The third force is \textbf{Computational Drag} (\(\Drag\)), which
captures the cost of computation, energy, memory, and any other
resource that the system consumes in the pursuit of its objectives.
Drag is an opposing force: it resists state changes that require
disproportionate resource expenditure, biasing the system toward
efficient, low-cost strategies.

Computational drag has no analog in classical optimization, where
resource costs are either ignored or treated as constraints external
to the objective function. But it is deeply natural from both a
biological and engineering perspective. Biological systems operate
under tight thermodynamic constraints: the brain consumes roughly
20 watts, a fact that has shaped the evolution of its algorithms
profoundly. The efficiency imperatives of biological computation---
sparse coding, predictive processing, hierarchical representations---
are all adaptive responses to the metabolic cost of neural activity
\citep{kim2022freeenergy}.

In \uvif{}, drag is formalized as a dissipative term in the dynamics:
\begin{equation}
  \Drag(\mathbf{s}, \dot{\mathbf{s}}) \propto
  \|\dot{\mathbf{s}}\|^2 \cdot C(\mathbf{s}),
  \label{eq:drag}
\end{equation}
where $C(\mathbf{s})$ is a state-dependent cost coefficient
that encodes the resource intensity of operating at state $\mathbf{s}$.
Computationally expensive states---those requiring large models, high
energy, or intensive memory---have high $C$, and the system is
naturally biased away from them when the epistemic and efficiency
benefits do not justify the cost.

\subsection{Action Efficiency Under Uncertainty}

The fourth force is \textbf{Action Efficiency} (\(\Eff\)), which
governs the system's selection of actions under uncertainty. Efficiency
here means not computational efficiency but \emph{policy quality}: the
system should take actions that move it toward its goals with minimal
wasted effort, avoiding both under-exploration (missing information
that would improve future decisions) and over-exploration (gathering
information at unnecessary cost).

This force connects \uvif{} to the epistemic-pragmatic balance studied
in active inference \citep{parr2022active,zhang2024fep_overview}.
In active inference, expected free energy combines epistemic value
(information gain) with pragmatic value (reward) to select actions.
\uvif{} embeds a similar logic within its force architecture: action
efficiency drives the system toward actions whose expected outcomes
align with the Variational Equilibrium, weighted by both their
informational and practical payoffs.

Formally:
\begin{equation}
  \Eff(\mathbf{s}, a) \propto
  \mathbb{E}_{p(\mathbf{s}'|a,\mathbf{s})}\bigl[V(\mathbf{s}') - C(a)\bigr],
  \label{eq:action_eff}
\end{equation}
where $V(\mathbf{s}')$ is the value of reaching state $\mathbf{s}'$
(in terms of proximity to Variational Equilibrium) and $C(a)$ is the
cost of action $a$.

\subsection{Sustainability and Long-Term Adaptive Stability}
\label{subsec:sustainability}

The fifth and perhaps most distinctive force in \uvif{} is
\textbf{Sustainability} (\(\Sus\)). This force represents the
system's drive to preserve the conditions that make its own continued
functioning possible over extended time horizons. It is the analog, in
artificial and organizational intelligence, of the ecological principle
of carrying capacity: the recognition that any system embedded in a
resource-constrained environment must modulate its behavior to avoid
depleting the conditions that sustain it.

In ecosystems, sustainability emerges from the coevolution of organisms
and environments: organisms that overexploit their resources go extinct,
while those that maintain the productivity of their environment persist
\citep{folke2006resilience,dyke2013environmental}. In organizations,
sustainability drives are reflected in long-term strategic planning,
stakeholder management, and the willingness to forgo short-term gains
to preserve long-term viability \citep{silvestre2020integrative}.

In a \uvif{} system, the sustainability field is a negative feedback
on behaviors that degrade the system's long-term operating environment:
\begin{equation}
  \Sus(\mathbf{s}) \propto
  -\nabla_{\mathbf{s}} \;\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{env}}(\mathbf{s}),
  \label{eq:sustainability}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{env}}(\mathbf{s})$ measures the rate of
environmental resource depletion induced by operating at state $\mathbf{s}$.
States that deplete resources rapidly are pushed against; states
that replenish or preserve resources are favored.

\subsection{Admissibility Constraints}
\label{subsec:admissibility}

The five forces jointly define a dynamical landscape, but the system
must also operate within a region of \emph{admissible} states---states
that satisfy hard constraints imposed by physical reality, ethical
norms, safety requirements, and institutional boundaries.

\begin{definition}[Admissibility Set]
  The admissibility set $\Adm \subset \mathcal{S}$ is the set of states
  satisfying:
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item \emph{Physical feasibility}: the state is achievable given the
      system's physical constitution and resource limits.
    \item \emph{Safety}: the state does not expose the system or its
      environment to unacceptable risk.
    \item \emph{Ethical alignment}: the state is consistent with the
      normative constraints governing the system's deployment context.
    \item \emph{Legality}: the state complies with applicable rules and
      regulations.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

Admissibility constraints are not forces in the dynamical sense; they
are hard boundaries. The force dynamics operate within $\Adm$, and
any trajectory that would leave $\Adm$ is reflected back by a
repulsive boundary potential. This structure ensures that Variational
Equilibria are always physically realizable and ethically acceptable,
not merely mathematically convenient.

\subsection{Variational Equilibrium as the Core Principle of Intelligence}

The five forces and the admissibility constraints together define the
\emph{landscape of intelligence} for a \uvif{} system. Variational
Equilibrium is the natural resting configuration of this landscape:
the point (or manifold) at which the competing forces are balanced,
the admissibility constraints are satisfied, and the system can sustain
its operation indefinitely.

This equilibrium is not static. As the external environment changes,
the force field changes, and the equilibrium shifts. The system tracks
the shifting equilibrium through the same dynamics that define it---
the force balance continuously recalibrates in response to environmental
feedback. This is the variational analog of adaptive homeostasis in
biology: not a fixed setpoint, but a setpoint that adapts to the
conditions it encounters \citep{davies2016adaptive,bich2025homeostasis}.

\begin{principle}[The Variational Equilibrium Principle]
\label{prin:veq2}
Intelligence, in the \uvif{} sense, is the capacity of a system
to maintain its state trajectory in the vicinity of Variational
Equilibrium across a wide range of internal and external perturbations,
while allowing the equilibrium itself to evolve in response to
persistent environmental changes.
\end{principle}

This principle subsumes both stability (the system returns to
equilibrium after perturbation) and adaptability (the equilibrium
shifts when the environment makes the current operating point
non-viable). It is, in other words, the formal expression of what
biologists call \emph{homeodynamics} \citep{lloyd2001homeodynamics}
and ecologists call \emph{adaptive resilience} \citep{folke2006resilience},
translated into the language of variational dynamics and applied to
the design of intelligent systems.

\subsection{Bounded Epistemic Acquisition}

The epistemic pull within UVIF continuously drives systems toward improved understanding, reduced uncertainty, and adaptive model refinement. However, this epistemic process is permanently bounded by incomplete observability, probabilistic environmental evolution, and finite resource constraints \citep{lieder2019resourcerational,griffiths2015rational}.

UVIF therefore rejects the assumption that intelligent systems can or should seek exhaustive certainty before acting. Excessive information acquisition may itself consume unsustainable computational, energetic, or temporal resources. The pursuit of maximal certainty may paradoxically reduce adaptive viability when delayed action introduces escalating systemic risk \citep{callaway2022rational}.

Consequently, epistemic intelligence in UVIF is not defined by complete certainty, but by the ability to maintain sufficiently informed adaptive operation under bounded knowledge conditions. The objective is viable equilibrium rather than exhaustive disclosure.

\subsection{Reasonable Action Under Uncertainty}

The action-efficiency force in UVIF does not seek perfectly optimal decisions under complete information. Instead, it seeks adaptive and sustainable action under constrained observability, finite time horizons, and evolving environmental conditions.

Natural systems routinely operate according to this principle. Biological organisms act before certainty is complete. Human cognition balances risk against delay. Ecological systems adapt incrementally under fluctuating conditions rather than through exhaustive predictive optimization \citep{schulkin2019allostasis,callaway2022rational}.

Within UVIF, intelligent action therefore corresponds to reasonable viability rather than theoretical perfection. A decision is considered intelligent not because it minimizes all possible future error, but because it preserves coherent adaptive functionality while maintaining long-term equilibrium across competing organizational forces.
