\section{Optimization, Regulation, and Viability}
\label{sec:optimization_equilibrium}

\subsection{What the critique of optimization does and does not imply}
Optimization provides a language for choosing actions or parameters under objectives and constraints. It does not inherently require a stationary environment, unlimited computation, complete information, or a single fixed goal. A scalar objective can encode multiple considerations, and multi-objective methods need not select one fixed scalarization. Likewise, sequential decision models can include resource stocks, irreversible events, and deployment horizons in their state and transition descriptions.

The relevant difficulty is consequently one of model specification and implementation. An objective that omits an important consequence can incentivize undesirable behaviour; a constraint that is incorrectly estimated can fail to protect the system. Safe reinforcement learning addresses such concerns through risk-sensitive objectives, constraints, and restrictions on exploration \citep{garcia2015saferl}. Alignment additionally raises questions about whose values and interests determine acceptable outcomes \citep{gabriel2020artificial}. Renaming these considerations as forces does not resolve either problem.

\uvif{} instead emphasizes explicit representation of regulatory demands. Its potential benefit is that designers can inspect which mechanisms acquire information, preserve internal structure, consume computation, produce useful action, and maintain future capacity. Whether this decomposition is useful must be assessed against alternatives that receive the same information, resources, and opportunities for adaptation.

\subsection{A motivating example: autonomous navigation}
Consider a mobile agent operating with limited battery capacity in an incompletely mapped environment. Additional sensing can reduce uncertainty but consumes time and energy. A short route can increase progress while reducing the reserve needed to recover from an obstacle or return to a charging station. Updating the map may improve navigation while introducing errors into previously reliable representations.

These trade-offs can be addressed through constrained planning or control. The \uvif{} description adds a proposed assignment of responsibilities: epistemic pull evaluates useful sensing; selective protection regulates changes to critical representations; computational drag accounts for inference expenditure; action efficiency evaluates progress and timeliness; sustainability tracks future resource availability. Collision avoidance remains a separately enforced admissibility requirement, not a preference that a sufficiently large information benefit can outweigh.

The example illustrates the distinction between a soft influence and a hard constraint. Reducing a risk penalty is a preference; remaining inside a verified safe operating set is a dynamical property. A controller may have to sacrifice progress or stop when no safe action exists. Neither the existence of five named components nor a small net force certifies that property.

\subsection{Bounded information and timely action}
Partial observability and limited computation motivate a balance between further inference and action. They do not require an appeal to quantum uncertainty or a general claim that all uncertainty is irreducible. Some uncertainty is reducible by measurement; some persists because relevant variables are hidden, observations are noisy, or future disturbances are unknown. Resource-rational models already ask when further computation is worth its cost \citep{lieder2019resourcerational,callaway2022rational}.

In \uvif{}, bounded information motivates measurable stopping and allocation rules. A useful implementation should specify when additional information changes the action enough to justify its acquisition cost and delay. An assertion that the system has achieved ``balance'' is inadequate unless the underlying quantities and decision rule are identified.
