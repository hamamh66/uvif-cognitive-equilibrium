\section{Variational Equilibrium in Natural and Artificial Cognitive Systems}
\label{sec:cognitive_systems}

The preceding sections developed \uvif{} as a general account of persistent
organization. This section narrows the focus to the case that motivates the
framework most directly and constrains it most sharply: cognition. Cognitive
systems are the setting in which the five forces are simultaneously observable,
in which competing formal accounts already exist, and in which the framework's
claims can therefore be made to do discriminating work rather than merely
descriptive work.

The argument proceeds in four steps. We first state what it means, concretely,
for a cognitive system to occupy a Variational Equilibrium. We then show that
three established research programmes in cognitive science---predictive
processing and active inference, resource-rational analysis, and allostatic
regulation---each capture a projection of that equilibrium onto a subset of the
forces, and that their well-known points of friction with one another arise
precisely where the omitted forces matter. We then turn to cognitive
architectures, asking what the framework implies for the design of integrated
cognitive systems. We close by stating the predictions that distinguish \uvif{}
from these neighbours, since a framework that reorganizes existing results
without generating new ones has not earned its place.

\subsection{Cognition as a Balance of Forces}
\label{subsec:cognition_forces}

Consider a cognitive system embedded in an environment it cannot fully observe,
equipped with finite metabolic and computational resources, and required to act
before its uncertainty has been resolved. Five pressures act on such a system at
every moment.

The \emph{epistemic pull} (\(\Epull\)) drives the acquisition of information
that would reduce uncertainty about the environment and about the consequences
of the system's own actions. In cognitive terms this is curiosity, exploratory
sampling, attention allocation, and deliberate hypothesis testing.

The \emph{protective shield} (\(\Prot\)) preserves internal states whose
disruption would be catastrophic or irreversible. In biological cognition this
includes the physiological variables that must remain within narrow bounds; in
cognitive terms it includes the stability of core representations, identity, and
commitments that are not renegotiated in response to every incoming observation.

The \emph{computational drag} (\(\Drag\)) is the cost of inference itself. Every
cognitive operation consumes time, energy, and working memory. This is not a
nuisance term: for a brain operating under a severe metabolic budget, the cost of
computing an answer is commensurate with the value of having it.

The \emph{efficiency imperative} (\(\Eff\)) demands that action occur within the
window in which it remains useful. A decision that is correct but late is, in
many ecologically realistic settings, indistinguishable in consequence from a
decision that is wrong.

The \emph{sustainability field} (\(\Sus\)) requires that the system's operating
policy remain feasible over horizons far longer than any single decision. A
cognitive strategy that is locally optimal but exhausts the resources on which
future cognition depends is not intelligent in the sense the framework intends.

\begin{definition}[Cognitive Variational Equilibrium]
\label{def:cog_veq}
A cognitive system occupies a Variational Equilibrium when its operating policy
lies on the manifold along which no single force can be further satisfied without
displacing the system beyond the admissibility constraints \(\Adm\) imposed by
the remaining forces. Cognition, on this account, is the continuous process of
navigating toward and along that manifold under perturbation.
\end{definition}

Two features of Definition~\ref{def:cog_veq} deserve emphasis, because they are
what separates the framework from a restatement of multi-objective optimization.
First, the equilibrium is a manifold rather than a point: there is in general a
continuum of admissible operating policies, and which one a system occupies is
underdetermined by the forces alone. This is a feature, not an indeterminacy to
be eliminated---it is what allows a cognitive system to shift its operating point
in response to context without a change of objective. Second, the forces are not
combined into a scalar by a fixed weighting. The relative influence of each force
is itself state-dependent: metabolic depletion raises the effective weight of
\(\Drag\), threat raises that of \(\Prot\), and a benign environment with slack
resources raises that of \(\Epull\). A system whose force weights were fixed in
advance would not be at Variational Equilibrium; it would be optimizing a
particular scalarization of it.

\subsection{Relation to Predictive Processing and Active Inference}
\label{subsec:pp_ai}

Predictive processing holds that the brain is fundamentally a hierarchical
prediction machine, continuously matching sensory input against top-down
expectation and propagating the residual error
\citep{clark2013whatever}. Active inference extends this into a theory of action:
an agent acts so as to bring about the observations its generative model
predicts, so that perception and action become two routes to the same end
\citep{friston2010fep,parr2022active,buckley2017fep,bruineberg2016anticipating}. The programme is the
closest existing kin of \uvif{}, and the resemblance is not superficial---both
are variational, both treat uncertainty reduction as constitutive rather than
instrumental, and both deny any clean separation between inference and action.

The difference lies in what is held fixed. Active inference derives behaviour
from the minimization of a single functional, expected free energy, within which
epistemic value (information gain) and pragmatic value (goal attainment) appear
as additive components. That unification is the framework's principal elegance,
and it is also, we argue, where it purchases parsimony at the cost of resolution.
Because the two contributions enter as terms in one quantity, the trade-off
between them is fixed by the form of the generative model and the precision
assigned to its priors. Whether an agent explores or exploits is thus a
consequence of how the model was specified, and an observed shift in that balance
must be explained by positing a corresponding shift in precision.

\uvif{} makes the opposite modelling choice. \(\Epull\) and \(\Eff\) are
separate forces whose relative influence is a state variable of the system rather
than a parameter of its model. The balance between them is an outcome of the
dynamics, not an input to them. This is a more permissive framework and
correspondingly a weaker one: it predicts less about any particular agent than
active inference does, and in exchange it does not require that every observed
change in exploratory behaviour be absorbed into the precision term. We take
this to be an honest trade rather than a strict improvement, and we return to
what would discriminate the two in Section~\ref{subsec:cog_predictions}.

The second and more consequential difference concerns cost. In the free-energy
formulation, the computational expense of inference is not itself represented in
the quantity being minimized; the agent minimizes free energy without the price
of that minimization appearing in the objective. For a theory of an organ that
consumes a fifth of the body's energy budget at rest, this omission is not
innocuous. \uvif{} promotes \(\Drag\) and \(\Sus\) to first-class forces
precisely so that the cost of thinking, and the long-run feasibility of
continuing to think, constrain the system's operating point directly. This is
the point at which the framework makes contact with resource-rational analysis.

\subsection{Relation to Resource-Rational Analysis}
\label{subsec:resource_rational}

Resource-rational analysis holds that human cognition is well described as the
optimal use of limited computational resources: the mind selects the cognitive
strategy that maximizes expected utility net of the cost of executing it
\citep{lieder2019resourcerational}. The programme has been notably successful in
turning apparent irrationality into evidence of rational adaptation---anchoring
effects, limited planning depth, and heuristic choice have each been recovered
as the resource-optimal policy for an agent with the constraints humans actually
have \citep{griffiths2015rational,callaway2022rational}.

Resource-rational analysis is, in the terms of this paper, an account of the
balance between \(\Eff\) and \(\Drag\), conducted with unusual rigour. Its
relation to \uvif{} is therefore complementary rather than competitive: it
supplies the formal machinery for two of the five forces, and the framework
supplies the context in which those two sit alongside three others.

What the framework adds is the observation that resource-rationality is defined
relative to a utility function whose specification is exogenous to the analysis,
and over a horizon that is typically the duration of the task. Both restrictions
matter. A strategy that is resource-optimal for the task at hand may be
unsustainable if adopted as a standing policy---the relevant failure is not
irrationality within the episode but the depletion of a resource the analysis
did not track. \(\Sus\) is the force that represents this, and it has no natural
home in a per-task resource-rational analysis. Similarly, \(\Prot\) captures the
asymmetry between recoverable and unrecoverable errors, which a utility function
can encode only by assigning a large negative value to the latter---a
representation that is formally adequate but obscures the qualitative difference
between an error that costs utility and one that ends the system's capacity to
accrue it.

\subsection{Relation to Homeostatic and Allostatic Regulation}
\label{subsec:allostasis}

The physiological literature supplies the framework's most direct empirical
anchor. Homeostasis describes the maintenance of critical internal variables
within viable bounds \citep{billman2020homeostasis}; adaptive homeostasis adds
that the bounds themselves shift in response to signals \citep{davies2016adaptive};
and homeodynamics recognizes that living systems exhibit organized transitions
between stable regimes rather than residence at a single set point
\citep{lloyd2001homeodynamics,petitjean2025homeodynamics,farnsworth2025setpoint}.

Allostasis carries this into cognition proper. On the allostatic account, the
brain does not merely correct deviations after they occur; it predicts the
demands the body is about to face and mobilizes resources in advance, accepting
the computational cost of prediction in exchange for a reduction in costly
regulatory error \citep{schulkin2019allostasis}. Neuroimaging evidence supports a
large-scale intrinsic brain system serving allostasis and interoception, linking
predictive regulation of the body to the same cortical machinery implicated in
predictive processing of the world \citep{kleckner2017evidence}.

This is, we suggest, Variational Equilibrium observed in a single system with all
five forces visible at once. Prediction of upcoming demand is \(\Epull\);
defence of viable physiological bounds is \(\Prot\); the acknowledged cost of
predictive computation is \(\Drag\); mobilization ahead of demand is \(\Eff\);
and the requirement that the regulatory strategy remain affordable across the
organism's lifespan is \(\Sus\). The allostatic literature has, in effect,
already described the framework's central object in one domain. What \uvif{}
proposes is that the same organizational form recurs at other scales, and the
force of that proposal rests on whether the recurrence is substantive or merely
analogical---a question we address directly in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{Implications for Cognitive Architectures}
\label{subsec:architectures}

Integrated cognitive architectures---ACT-R, Soar, Sigma, and the several dozen
others that remain in active development---represent the field's most sustained
attempt to specify a complete cognitive system rather than an isolated mechanism
\citep{kotseruba201840}. The standard model of the mind articulates the
consensus these architectures have converged upon regarding structure,
processing, memory, and learning \citep{laird2017standard}.

That consensus is, however, largely a consensus about \emph{mechanism}: what
modules exist, what representations they exchange, how control passes among
them. It says comparatively little about what governs the allocation of a
system's finite capacity among competing demands at run time, which is
ordinarily handled by architecture-specific conflict resolution or utility
mechanisms. \uvif{} is not a competing architecture and does not propose one.
It is a specification of the regulatory layer such an architecture requires:
a statement of which pressures must be represented and balanced, leaving open
the mechanisms by which any particular architecture implements them.

Three design consequences follow. First, an architecture that represents the
cost of its own inference---not merely as a scheduling constraint but as a
quantity entering its control decisions---will exhibit the graceful degradation
under load that fixed-policy architectures lack. Second, an architecture with an
explicit \(\Prot\) force will distinguish recoverable from unrecoverable
failure, and will decline actions of the latter kind under uncertainty rather
than treating all errors as commensurable losses. Third, an architecture
carrying an explicit \(\Sus\) force will admit policies that are locally
suboptimal in order to remain feasible over its deployment lifetime---a property
that no per-episode objective can express. These are design implications, not
results; the framework as it stands motivates them but does not demonstrate
them.

\subsection{Discriminating Predictions}
\label{subsec:cog_predictions}

A framework that merely reorganizes existing findings has limited value. We
therefore state, as hypotheses rather than established results, the predictions
by which \uvif{} could be separated from the accounts discussed above.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(H\arabic*),leftmargin=2.2em]
  \item \textbf{State-dependent force weighting.} If the balance between
    epistemic and pragmatic drives is a state variable rather than a model
    parameter, then manipulating a subject's resource state---metabolic,
    temporal, or computational---while holding the task's information structure
    and reward structure fixed should shift exploratory behaviour. An expected
    free energy account can accommodate such a shift only by positing a
    concurrent change in precision, so the discriminating test is whether the
    shift occurs without independent evidence of a precision change.

  \item \textbf{Sustainability effects beyond the episode.} If \(\Sus\) is a
    genuine force rather than a long-horizon utility term, then agents informed
    that a task regime will be sustained indefinitely should adopt systematically
    different---and locally suboptimal---strategies from agents facing an
    otherwise identical single episode, even when the stated payoff structure is
    held constant.

  \item \textbf{Asymmetric treatment of irreversibility.} If \(\Prot\)
    enters as an admissibility constraint---a ceiling on tolerated exposure
    to unrecoverable outcomes---rather than as a term in the objective, then
    choices involving such outcomes should not be reproducible by any
    monotone rescaling of the utilities attached to recoverable ones with a
    \emph{finite} coefficient. We state the prediction in this restricted
    form deliberately. A sufficiently steep penalty approximates a hard
    constraint arbitrarily well, and converges to it as the coefficient
    diverges; the unrestricted claim---that no monotone rescaling can
    reproduce constrained choice---is therefore false as a limit statement.
    What is testable is the finite case: whether observed choices are fit by
    a penalty whose estimated coefficient remains bounded as the range of
    admitted values is widened, or whether that estimate diverges.

  \item \textbf{Characteristic failure signatures.} Selectively suppressing a
    single force should produce a distinguishable failure mode: suppressing
    \(\Epull\) should yield confident action on a stale world model;
    suppressing \(\Prot\) should yield fluent behaviour punctuated by
    catastrophic error; suppressing \(\Sus\) should yield performance that is
    strong initially and degrades monotonically with deployment duration.
\end{enumerate}

These predictions are qualitative, and the framework does not yet specify the
functional forms that would make them quantitative. Establishing those forms is
the principal item on the research agenda set out in
Section~\ref{sec:future}.

A reference implementation of the five forces, together with a simulation of the
four predictions stated above, is openly available in the repository
accompanying this article \citep{Hamam2026GitHub}. Each force is instantiated as
an explicit function of a policy and an environment state, and each prediction is
evaluated against a comparator drawn from the accounts discussed in this
section---a fixed-weight scalarization for (H1), a per-episode objective for
(H2), a monotone utility penalty for (H3), and the intact force set for (H4).
Comparator parameters are fitted on calibration environments disjoint from those
on which results are reported, and comparator search ranges are widened until the
fit ceases to improve, so that no reported difference rests on having given the
alternative too little room. Within that model, (H1), (H2) and (H4) hold and are
robust to perturbation of every model constant, while (H3) holds only in the
restricted finite-coefficient form stated above: the fitted penalty coefficient
grows without bound as the search range widens, which is the signature of a
penalty approximating a constraint rather than reproducing it.

These are results about a model whose functional forms are stipulated rather than
measured. They establish that each prediction is well posed, that the signature
it names is measurable, and that the framework generates that signature where a
natural comparator does not. They are not evidence about the behaviour of
biological or artificial cognitive systems, and they do not stand in for the
empirical tests described in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

A companion study takes up a narrower empirical
question within this programme---what happens when the decision threshold of a
deployed classification system is set without regard to the review capacity that
must absorb its borderline cases---and reports an analysis of confidence-based
human review under fixed operating conditions \citep{hamam2026thresholds}. That
study is empirical and institution-facing; the present paper is the conceptual
framework from which its question is drawn. Neither depends on the other for its
claims.
